  could be drug targets? This is a BT3041 (IIT Madras) team project built on
  data from Ziegler et al. 2021.

  The data

  - Nasal-swab single-cell RNA-seq: 32,588 cells from 58 donors.
  - Donor groups: 15 healthy controls, 14 mild/moderate COVID, 21 severe COVID,
    6 ICU patients without COVID, and 2 convalescent donors (excluded from all
    analyses).
  - The main task is mild (14) vs severe (21). Ziegler's finding is that
    patients who go on to severe disease have a weaker early interferon
    (antiviral) response in the nasal epithelium.

  The central constraint

  There are only 35 COVID patients, so the patient is the sample, never the 
  cell. Cells from one person are not independent. That one rule drives the main
  design choices:
  - Cross-validation splits by patient.
  - Anything learned from data (scaling, gene selection, PCA) is fit only on the
    training patients.
  - Tests and p-values are at patient level, with BH-FDR correction.
  - Every plot states how many patients it shows.

❯ explain all the concepts required and involved in each step in simple terms   

⏺ Background ideas first

  scRNA-seq. Each cell is broken open and the RNA molecules inside are counted.
  The result is a table with cells as rows and about 31,000 genes as columns. A
  count says how active that gene was in that cell.

  Cell types. Nasal swabs contain ciliated, secretory, goblet, basal and
  squamous cells, plus immune cells such as T cells and macrophages. The authors
  labelled each cell's type. We reuse their labels instead of re-annotating.

  Pseudoreplication. Thousands of cells come from just 58 people, and cells from
  one person resemble each other. If you treat cells as independent samples you
  get fake confidence, with tiny p-values that don't mean anything. So the
  person is the unit of analysis.

  Step 01: QC and normalisation (Vijay)

  - QC means dropping bad cells: too few genes detected (empty droplets), too
    many (two cells stuck together), or a high share of mitochondrial RNA (a
    dying cell). The authors already filtered, so here nothing is removed and
    the metrics are only recorded.
  - Normalisation. Some cells are sequenced deeper, which inflates their counts.
    Rescale each cell to 10,000 total counts, then take log(1+x). The log stops
    a few very highly expressed genes from dominating.
  - HVGs (highly variable genes). Pick the 2,000 genes that vary most between
    cells, since those carry the signal. Viral genes are excluded so we study
    the host's response.

  Step 02: embedding and clustering (Vijay)

  - PCA compresses 2,000 genes into a few "axes of variation".
  - UMAP squashes that into a 2-D picture where similar cells sit close
    together.
  - Leiden clustering groups similar cells automatically.
  - ARI (adjusted Rand index) measures how well our clusters agree with the
    authors' labels. 1 is perfect and 0 is random. It's a sanity check on the
    labels.

  Step 03: patient-level features (Sahaj)

  This turns 32,588 cells into 58 rows, one per patient.
  - Composition (C3). What fraction of each person's cells are ciliated,
    secretory, and so on. Proportions always sum to 1, so a rise in one type
    forces others down. The CLR transform (centred log-ratio) fixes this by
    taking logs relative to the person's average, with a pseudocount of 0.5 so
    zeros don't break the log.
  - Pseudobulk (C4). Add up all of one patient's cells (or one cell type's) into
    a single profile of raw counts. This gives one number per gene per patient,
    and the sample size is then honestly 58.
  - C2 is the metadata: group, sex, age.

  Step 04: composition tests (Sahaj)

  - Kruskal–Wallis compares 3 groups without assuming a bell curve. Mann–Whitney
    U compares 2 groups the same way. Both are rank-based, so a few extreme
    patients don't dominate.
  - Multiple testing. Test 17 cell types and about one will look significant by
    luck. BH-FDR adjusts the p-values to q-values, so you control the share of
    false discoveries among your hits.

  Step 05: classification (yours)

  - Classification. Learn a rule from features to a label (mild or severe).
  - Models. Logistic regression is a weighted sum turned into a probability.
    Linear SVM finds the line that best separates the groups. Random forest is
    many decision trees voting, which handles non-linear patterns.
  - Regularisation (L2, the C parameter). It penalises extreme weights so the
    model doesn't memorise 35 patients.
  - Class weight "balanced". 21 severe vs 14 mild would bias a model toward
    "severe", so errors on the smaller group are weighted up.
  - Cross-validation. Hide some patients, train on the rest, predict the hidden
    ones. 5-fold does this five times so everyone is hidden once. Repeating it
    with 10 different random splits shows how much the answer depends on luck.
    Leave-one-patient-out hides one person at a time.
  - Data leakage. Any step that learns from data (scaling, picking genes, PCA)
    must be fit on the training patients only. If the test patient influences
    those steps, the score is inflated. That's why they sit inside the pipeline.
  - AUROC. Pick a random severe and a random mild patient. AUROC is the chance
    the model scores the severe one higher. 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is
    perfect. Balanced accuracy is the average of the hit rate on each group.
  - Permutation test. Shuffle the severe/mild labels, rerun everything, and
    repeat 1000 times. That shows what AUROC you'd get from pure noise. The
    p-value is the fraction of shuffles that did as well as the real result. It
    answers "is this better than chance?"
  - Feature sets. comp is composition. pb_all is the pseudobulk reduced to its
    top-variance genes and then PCA, inside each fold. comb is both.

  Step 06: ISG baseline (Sidharth)

  - ISGs are interferon-stimulated genes, the antiviral alarm. The paper's
    headline is that severe patients mount a weaker early alarm. A single "ISG
    score" per patient is the simple baseline that any fancier model has to
    beat.

  Steps 07–08: differential expression and enrichment (Sahaj)

  - DE (differential expression). For each gene, is it higher or lower in severe
    than mild? Pseudobulk DE (pydeseq2) does this with one sample per patient,
    which avoids the pseudoreplication problem. DESeq2 models counts as a
    negative binomial distribution, a good fit for noisy counts.
  - Enrichment. If your 200 hit genes keep landing in "interferon response",
    that's a pathway story. Over-representation uses Fisher's exact test: more
    overlap with a pathway than chance. GSEA ranks all genes and asks whether a
    pathway's genes cluster at the top or bottom.

  Step 09: attribution (yours, next)

  - Single-cell-type models. Train on ciliated cells only, secretory only, and
    so on. A type whose model beats its own permutation null carries signal.
  - Ablation. Train on everything, then remove one cell type at a time. If AUROC
    falls a lot, that type mattered. The drop is ΔAUROC.
  - Gene importance. Logistic regression coefficients show each gene's weight.
    SHAP splits a prediction into per-gene contributions. Stability is how often
    a gene lands in the top 50 across the CV fits, which tells you whether it's
    reliable or a fluke.

  Steps 10–13: after the sprint

  - Key-gene shortlist (10). Genes that are significant in DE and also important
    in the model.
  - Sensitivity analysis (11). Redo the analysis while changing choices, such as
    the minimum cells per pseudobulk or the QC thresholds. If the conclusion
    survives, it's robust.
  - Perturbation (12). Nudge a gene's expression up or down in the data, run the
    trained classifier, and see how the predicted severity moves. It's a cheap
    in-silico "what if we drugged this?"
  - Target prioritisation (13). Score candidate genes on whether they're
    druggable (enzyme, receptor and so on), have existing compounds (DGIdb),
    have prior COVID evidence (Open Targets), are safe (HPA tissue expression),
    and show a perturbation effect.

  Steps 14–15: validation (Vijay)

  Check our findings against what Ziegler reported, and against a second,
  independent cohort (Chua et al.). Replication on new patients is the strongest
  evidence.

  The honest caveat that runs through everything

  With 35 patients, one person shifts AUROC by about 0.03, so results are noisy.
  That's why we repeat CV and run permutation tests, and why the exploration
  could not honestly reach 0.90. Choosing the best of many models on the same
  data inflates the score, which is why I used nested CV there.

⏺ What the data is

  One big table: 32,588 rows (cells) × ~31,700 columns (genes). Each number is
  how many RNA molecules of that gene were detected in that cell. Each cell also
  has labels: which person it came from (donor_id, 58 people), that person's
  group (control, mild, severe), and the authors' cell type (ciliated, secretory
  and so on).

  The classifier needs one row per person, because the person is the unit we
  predict. So the cells have to be collapsed into one vector per person. There
  are two ways to do that, and they give two different vectors.

  Vector 1: composition (17 numbers per patient)

  Ask which kinds of cells this person has.
  1. Count each person's cells by type, for example 800 ciliated, 300 secretory,
     40 T cells.
  2. Convert to proportions, then to CLR (log of each proportion relative to the
     person's average log-proportion). A pseudocount of 0.5 is added so a zero
     count doesn't give log(0).
  3. Result: 17 numbers per person, one per cell type. These are the clr__*
     columns in C3.

  This captures who is there, not what they're doing.

  Vector 2: pseudobulk (thousands of numbers, then compressed to 10)

  Ask what the person's cells are expressing.
  1. Take all of one person's cells and add their counts gene by gene. That
     gives ~31,700 numbers per person. It's as if you'd sequenced a bulk sample
     from them, hence "pseudobulk". This is the "all" row in C4.
  2. Convert to log counts-per-million (rescale by total counts so a deeply
     sequenced person isn't inflated, then log).
  3. 31,700 numbers for 35 patients is far too many, so it's shrunk inside each 
     CV fold:
     - Keep the 2,000 genes that vary most across the training patients.
     - Standardise each gene (subtract the mean, divide by the standard
       deviation).
     - PCA to 10 components. PCA finds the 10 combinations of those 2,000 genes
       that capture the most variation between patients. Each patient ends up
       with 10 numbers.

  Your question: are the three feature sets concatenated or run separately?

  They are the three feature sets in the script: comp, pb_all, comb. I think
  this is what you mean by the three methods, but tell me if you meant something
  else.

  - comp and pb_all run separately. Each gets its own models, its own scores,
    its own permutation tests.
  - comb is the concatenation. It joins the two: 17 composition numbers plus the
    10 pseudobulk PCs, so 27 numbers per patient. The 17 composition values are
    only standardised. The pseudobulk values go through the gene filter,
    standardisation and PCA described above, and then the two blocks are stacked
    side by side.

  The three models (logistic regression, SVM, random forest) are a separate
  axis. Every feature set is run through each model, giving 3 × 3 = 9
  combinations per task.

  Standardisation, and why it's inside the pipeline

  "Standardising" means putting every feature on the same scale (mean 0, spread
  1), so one feature doesn't dominate just because its numbers are bigger. The
  mean and spread must be computed from the training patients only. If the test
  patients help compute them, information leaks and the score is inflated. So
  standardisation, gene filtering and PCA are all fitted fresh inside each fold,
  on the training patients, and then applied to the held-out ones.

  One picture

  cells (32,588 × 31,700)
     │ group by person
     ├─ count cell types ──► CLR ──► 17 numbers ───────────────► comp
     │
     └─ sum counts per gene ──► log CPM ──► 31,700 numbers
                                (in each fold: top 2000 → standardise → PCA)
                                           ──► 10 numbers ──────► pb_all

  comb = [17 comp numbers | 10 pb numbers] = 27 numbers per patient

  Each patient is one row of 17, 10 or 27 numbers, and the label is mild (0) or
  severe (1). With 35 patients, that is a table of 35 rows × 17, 10 or 27
  columns.

  The gene counts follow this path in pb_all and comb:
  1. Add the counts of all of one patient's cells, gene by gene, giving ~31,700
     numbers per patient.
  2. Convert them to log counts-per-million.
  3. Inside each CV fold, keep the 2,000 most variable genes, standardise them,
     and run PCA down to 10 components.
  4. The model is trained on those 10 numbers.

  So the genes do reach the model, but not one by one. The model sees 10
  "gene-expression axes", each a weighted blend of the 2,000 genes. I did it
  this way because 35 patients with 2,000 or 31,700 separate gene columns would
  overfit badly. The model would have more columns than patients and could
  memorise noise.

  This has a cost. After PCA you can't say "gene X predicts severity", only
  "this blend of genes does". That is fine for the sprint question of whether
  severity is predictable at all.

  The gene-level question comes later:
  - Step 07 (DE) tests every gene individually between mild and severe.
  - Step 09, gene importance (yours, next) trains models where the genes stay as
    separate features, using coefficients and SHAP to rank them.

  Those steps are where the individual gene counts get used directly. In the
  sprint classifier, composition does most of the work and the pseudobulk adds a
  little. For example, mild vs severe random forest scores 0.83 on comp and
  0.80 on pb_all.

  p<0.05. The ablation agrees: random forest AUROC changes by −0.03 to +0.004
  when a type is dropped, which is smaller than the ±0.04 run-to-run noise.

  Why I'd read this as "not detected" rather than "not there":
  - Each type has only 17–30 patients, which is little power.
  - The combined linear models (logistic regression and SVM) overfit and sit at
    or below chance, with 0.45–0.48 AUROC. That's about 32 features for 28
    training patients. So their ablations tell us nothing, and I made random
    forest the primary model for the contract file. I chose 3 PCs per block
    after a quick 3-vs-10 comparison, so that's a design choice made after
    seeing results. Both choices are in the log.
  - Composition alone gets 0.74–0.83 (from the classifier runs), so the signal
    may sit in cell-type proportions rather than in per-type gene expression.
    This step doesn't test that directly.
